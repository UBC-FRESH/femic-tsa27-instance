# TSA 27 interim attribution of the AAC-to-harvest gap

**Status:** interim evidence synthesis, 29 October 2026. This is not a final attribution.
**Question:** why has the ratio of scaled volume to AAC fallen since 2017, and how much is
due to caribou habitat policy?

The first correction to the premise: the ratio did **not** decline smoothly from 2017. It
rebounded to 93% in 2018, collapsed in 2019–2021, recovered in 2022, dipped in 2023, and
recovered strongly in 2024–2025. A 2017 structural break is not supported by the annual data.

## What is already attributable

### Caribou is a real but bounded AAC effect

The TSR record gives quantitative caribou sensitivities:

- TSR4 2010, adding back incremental caribou reserves: **+8.4% short/mid**, **+2.2% long-term**.
- TSR4 2010, removing mature/old biodiversity constraints in caribou LUs: **+4.0% short/mid**,
  **+2.5% long-term**.
- TSR2 1999 analog, removing all caribou guidelines: **+8.2% long-term**.
- TSR5 2022 gives a much larger legal UWR footprint: **116,820 ha gross / 62,892 ha net**,
  29.1% of AFLB, but the 2022 AAC and base case are not published.

The current WS4 first-period capacity result — UWR harvest down 43.6% — is **not** an AAC
result. It removes the entire UWR footprint and uses a legacy THLB of 79,102 ha versus the
TSR5 preliminary 51,454 ha.

A defensible interim statement: caribou policy plausibly explains **roughly 5–15% of AAC**
based on the published TSR4/TSR2 sensitivities, with an unquantified additional effect from
the larger 2022 UWR/core/matrix direction. That is not enough to explain a 40–60 percentage
point collapse in the harvest ratio.

Relative to the 95,858 ha operable block area, the largest years are about **1.5–1.7%**. The
largest operable volume at risk is about 99,000 m³ in 2023, roughly 0.4% of the 24.22M m³
operable pool. This is a screen, not a volume-loss or salvage estimate, but it is far too
small to explain the 2019–2021 or 2023 collapses by itself.

### The annual snapshots add two timing constraints

The annual overlay in `reports/tsa27-annual-snapshots.md` adds:

- UWR u-3-005 rises by **10,601 ha (+10.0%)** in 2019, the same year harvest falls from 93% to
  49% of AAC. That is the clearest legal land-base timing event in the series.
- Cutblock area in 2019–2021 is only **8% below** 2017–2018, while harvest falls 24%; the 2025
  recovery to 83% of AAC uses less than half the 2018 cutblock area. A simple cutblock-area
  shortage does not fit the data.
- Active FTEN road length falls only **7.9%** from 2017 to 2026, a gradual access tightening
  rather than a collapse.
- The 2019 UWR add-back removes **1.223M m³ of total operable volume** and **0.841M m³ of
  positive-margin volume**, about **9.8%** of the pre-2019 positive pool. That is a real but
  bounded effect, not a 47% one-year collapse.
- Fire with no salvage removes at most **47,385 m³** in a year, under **0.6%** of the positive
  pool. See `reports/tsa27-year-by-year-screen.md`.
- The BC interior lumber AMV **still fails to reproduce the 2021–2023 path**. In 2021 the AMV
  factor is 1.98× the 2026 level but HBS is the lowest year at 30.2%; 2023 is 1.17× with HBS at
  42.5%. The AMV does fit 2019 (0.878×, HBS 49.1%) and 2025 (1.224×, HBS 83.4%). This points to
  a quantity/processing constraint in 2021–2023, not a price constraint. See
  `reports/tsa27-price-index-screen.md`.

### Processing capacity and undercut are the leading alternative explanations

The public record shows more than 30 BC sawmill closures since 2017, with further closures
and curtailments through 2025–2026. The local Revelstoke cluster still includes Downie Timber
and Selkirk Cedar, but there is no dated TSA 27 mill-capacity table in this instance.

The AAC denominator is fixed at 225,000 m³. The numerator is actual scaled volume. Since 2011
the average realized harvest is 197,219 m³, about **80% of AAC**. The 2019–2021 values are
30–49%, and 2023 is 42%. Those are undercut years, not AAC reductions. COVID, mill
curtailments, weak markets, haul/road costs, and limited accessible stands are all consistent
with that pattern; the current data do not apportion them.

The layer-by-layer synthesis is in `reports/tsa27-counterfactual-synthesis.md`.

## Interim answer

As well as the public record can support:

1. **Caribou policy is a secondary contributor**, plausibly in the **5–15% of AAC** range from
   the published TSR sensitivities, with the 2022 UWR/core/matrix package still unquantified.
2. **Fire overlap is small** relative to the THLB, though 2021 and 2023 salvage and access
   effects need a volume-level test.
3. **The ratio collapse is mostly undercut relative to AAC**, driven by processing capacity,
   markets, and operational access. The 2019–2021 and 2023 lows coincide with the period of
   mill closures/curtailments and weak lumber markets; the 2022 and 2024–2025 rebounds show
   the AAC itself did not collapse.

This is a bounded answer, not a precise decomposition. The next section says what is needed to
turn it into one.

## Maximum public-data programme

### Phase 1 — historical snapshots, not one current estate

Build annual 2017–2026 block snapshots with:

- VRI/cutblock history and age roll-back;
- TSR4/TSR5 land-base sequences;
- UWR 2010, 2014, and 2020 boundaries;
- OGMA/MOGMA, seral, and immature deferral layers;
- BCWS fire perimeters and burn severity;
- existing and new road access.

Without historical snapshots, a current-dataset model cannot attribute past harvest.

### Phase 2 — product, price, and cost ledger

Build one public, dated table per year for:

- species/grade log prices at plausible mills;
- interior stumpage rates;
- contractor and machine rates;
- planning/layout, loading/unloading, hauling, and road costs;
- mill capacity, accepted products, closures, and curtailments.

### Phase 3 — full-TSA accessibility screen

Do not solve a single 37,000-block MILP. Build a reduced-form block/year screen:

```text
legal eligibility
  -> physical access and system assignment
  -> delivered cost (harvest + road + haul + stumpage + loading)
  -> mill-gate revenue
  -> profitable volume under mill capacity
```

This screen can run across all blocks and years. Calibrate it to HBS annual totals and to
the sample operating areas.

### Phase 4 — FHOPS validation in operating areas

Run the tactical/operational FHOPS model on two or three operating areas, not the whole TSA.
Use it to validate the reduced-form cost and capacity assumptions, not to set the TSA total.

### Phase 5 — nested counterfactuals

Report a range, not a single number:

| Counterfactual | What it isolates |
|---|---|
| Actual HBS | observed |
| No caribou layers | legal caribou effect |
| No fire | fire/salvage effect |
| No road/terrain constraints | physical access effect |
| No mill-capacity/curtailment events | processing effect |
| No cost/price gap | economic effect |
| No COVID/market shock | demand effect |

Use sequential removal and report the range across orderings. The effects are not additive.

### Phase 6 — validation

Validate against:

- HBS annual totals 2017–2026;
- TSR4 caribou sensitivities (+8.4%/+2.2%, +4.0%/+2.5%);
- TSR2 +8.2% long-term;
- known fire years and mill closures;
- annual harvested area and species mix where available.

## What to do first

1. Build the dated mill-capacity and road-access tables. They are the highest-value missing
   inputs and the leading alternative explanation.
2. Build annual block snapshots for 2017–2026.
3. Build the full-TSA economic screen with explicit cost categories.
4. Validate a small number of operating areas in FHOPS.
5. Publish the attribution as a range with data gaps named, not as a point estimate.
